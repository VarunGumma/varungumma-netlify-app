A Single Sheet of Paper Cannot Decide Your Future!

Notes and lessons from my 2024 MS/PhD application season.

⌄

Introduction

With the MS/PhD application cycle around the corner this year (2025), I would like to share some of my experiences and learnings from my time. Around a year ago, I was in the cycle, almost as confused (if not more) as many of you are right now. The application cycle is a tedious, time-consuming, and uncertain process, which can be quite stressful if not handled in time. Most of the US, Canada, UK, and European universities open their PhD and MS application window around mid-September and have a hard deadline around mid-December (before Christmas), with European Universities closing much earlier by October end. University Admission committees and individual professors review your package and potentially call you for a personal interview before offering admission. Most of the insights I will share in this blog will be for a PhD in NLP and AI for which I applied in 2024; however, some of them apply to MS applications, which follow the same suit to some extent. Unfortunately, given the boom of AI, these domains have become extremely competitive, and in terms of the recent federal fund cuts, the recruitment has gotten even more stringent in the US.

The primary motivation for me to write this blog was to share my learnings from the process. As I did not have anyone to warn me about all the loopholes and road bumps in this process, I found it quite confusing and scary, as a single mistake could lead to your application being rejected. I hope the current applicants find this useful and avoid the mistakes I made. In the entire process, time is of the utmost essence, and I really urge you all to start as early as possible. Do not rush here, take this lightly or for granted, as it is probably the most important step if you are aiming for a PhD. I would repeat this multiple times in the blog: Your application cycle is unique to you, so do not copy or force-fit yourself to someone else's experiences and style.

An application package has a Statement of Purpose, Personal Statement, Letters of Recommendation, Test Scores, and your CV. I will discuss each of them below, with some additional tips. So stay tuned till the end!

Statement of Purpose (SoP) Clear vision and motivations for graduate study.

A professor or applications review committee cannot personally interview and speak to every student who applied, hence the statement of purpose. Your statement should convey your motivation, enthusiasm, and, mainly, potential research directions you might want to explore during your PhD based on your previous experiences. Statements are expected to be about 1000-1200 words long, with a hard limit of two pages on the content. I understand that you might have a lot to share and mention in your SoP, but do not cheat by narrowing the margins and font size (I did that, and it does not look good). Just stick to Times New Roman with a font size of 11. You can choose to write in a Google Doc, Word Doc, or use an Overleaf Latex Template (the latter is easier as it helps you cite papers cleanly). Keep a margin of 1in on both sides. The citations can flow beyond the two pages, and no one bothers.

cs-sop.org is a good database of statements from previous students who received admits from various top universities. Give them a read, but do not overfit to them in any way, and force-align yourself to any domain or problem mentioned there just for the sake of it. The whole admission cycle is about you, your learning, and your future ambitions, and it is okay (and expected) for different people to find different topics, research problems interesting. Similarly, there is no hard rule on the format of your SoP, except for the page formatting I mentioned earlier. You can choose to present your story in any way to wish as long as it is technically sound, understandable, and genuine. Students usually tend to cite their previous works in the SoP in a way to convey their publication list and also connect the ideas presented and discussed in the SoP to their previous work. What most professors reading a SoP look for is the relevance of your previous work, connection to proposed ideas, technical soundness, alignment with their lab's research, and your enthusiasm and belief in your work. I understand all this is a lot to pack into 1000-1200 words, but that's what Engineering is! Remember, you don't have to show off and connect all your works in the draft; your CV will take care of it.

More often than not, you may find yourself at a fork in the road where you would like to highlight multiple disjoint works in your SoP. This is a very tricky situation as the flow of the content can be disrupted. The best solution here would be to discuss this with your mentors and try to explain it under one umbrella, and use all their learnings to align and corroborate with your new proposals. Your SoP should not focus too much on your previous work and its technical details, but should discuss your proposed ideas and hypothesis while cleanly building upon your previous work. That connection is the key!

In a rare case, when you want to jump ship and move to a new domain, you have to be extra careful. Principal Investigators (PIs) usually tend to hire students who have some experience in their domain and can quickly pick up the pace in the lab. However, it is not an impossible feat. There have been several cases when students have been recruited for new domains (including myself). So, you need to structure your SoP (and your LoR) to make the Professor believe that you can learn their domain quickly, and can connect it to your fundamentals. It is better to be armed with the recent publications of the PI and discuss how you can improve and build on their work in the statement. This gives the PI some confidence that they don't need to invest too much time in you to teach you the bare fundamentals of their work. Your statement should also clearly indicate your other technical skills and experiences, which you can leverage for such a shift.

I understand this leads you to think, “Should I overfit to the PIs' domain in the SoP, or should I go about describing my work and ideas?” Many times, professors (especially the new ones) are liberal to let their new students bring their own topics and work on them by themselves. But an established research group with a set goal and funding might be restrictive. So, choose your PIs wisely according to your intention. If you are heavily inclined to work with a specific PI, then zero in on their work and build your statement around it. But if you wish to explore and apply more broadly, go with the latter. The former is also slightly risky, as your target space is smaller and other PIs might not connect to it, but again, I have had friends who have done both.

Most SoPs end with a conclusion paragraph where the students are expected to mention why they are applying to that particular university and professors. Keep this concise and to the point, and do not waste too many words here. Most students have a template with their technical content the same across SoPs for various universities, and just change the last paragraph. Although this may save you time, the forced stitch might break the flow of the reader. The biggest folly one can commit here is to put down the incorrect university and professors' names in a hurry (Ex, you are applying to Harvard, but forgot the change the last paragraph, and it states Stanford. Yes, this is more common than you think!) Don't worry, this is not the admissions committee routes your package to the relevant PIs. Every application process requires you to mention 3-5 professors on their website while filling in your details. However, the best and most elegant approach would be to write different SoPs for different universities, focusing on their PIs and core domains, while connecting your work to them uniquely. I know this is a lot of work when you are applying to 15-16 universities, but no pain, no gain!

There are many better blogs from previous students who got admitted, and they share their SoP writing experience in detail, and I don't have anything different to say except that it is your story and you get to decide how you wish to tell it. Any sort of plagiarism is also viewed seriously and rejected. Revise and refine your SoP as many times as possible; it is a painfully iterative process. Try to keep the usage of AI as minimal as possible and maintain the human touch. Get your SoP reviewed by as many people as possible, starting with your immediate mentors, who might help you refine your problem statements and technical details. Secondly, share them with the students of that particular university or professor to check if the statement aligns with their lab. In most cases, it is those students who conduct an initial review of the SoPs for further shortlisting, so it would be valuable to take in their inputs. But remember, too many cooks spoil the broth. After a while, you might notice orthogonal or highly niche comments. Be careful not to include anything that disrupts your original story.

UK and European universities also refer to the SoP as a Research statement, which is expected to be much longer and denser (3-5 pages) with a very detailed description of your problem statements and potential solutions. This is almost like a short paper where you attempt to motivate the problem and your hypothesis. Some professors later use it to apply for funding for your project.

Make sure your SoP is modular to some extent, and that you can fold it and compress it without losing much information and story. I remember UCLA had a textbox with a 500-word maximum word count for the SoP, unlike other universities, which ask you to submit a PDF.

MS statements are slightly different from PhD statements, where you discuss what interests you in that school. Since you are not targeting any professor for MS, your statement should address the entire school/department, and your motivations to pursue MS there. This kind of blends the line between SoP and Personal Statement as well. Since I did not apply for an MS abroad, take this advice with a bunch of salt (yes, not a pinch), but my general understanding is that MS applications focus on a Personal statement, while PhD on a SoP.

Personal Statement Your unique journey, challenges, and growth.

In addition to the Statement of Purpose, the university might also expect you to provide a Personal statement. This a bit ambiguous as some universities use these terms interchangeably, while others call the SoP a Research Statement/Proposal. A personal statement is mostly a non-technical document of about 500 words describing you, your journey, life lessons, and motivation to join University XYZ to pursue higher studies. This document should make the admissions committee believe that you have not randomly chosen to pursue a PhD/MS and University XYZ, but have some sense of what you wish to learn, what you can give back, how you align with the culture and beliefs of the university, and how this step aligns with your final life goals and ambitions.

Test Scores Standardized tests and language proficiency.

If you are applying from India, universities usually ask for an English proficiency test or certificate. Most of the well-accepted test scores are TOEFL, IELTS, and GRE. However, this can be waived off if you have an official statement on your degree transcript or certificate from your university, which states that English was the medium of instruction at that institution. You can upload this certificate and wait for it to get verified, which can take anywhere from a week to a couple of weeks. Choose wisely, as if this document is rejected by the university, your application could be desk-rejected if no other English language proficiency score is reported.

Post COVID, GRE scores for PhD applications are not valued, but can be self-reported. So, if you don't need it, don't give the exam and spend money and unnecessary effort on it! Remember, all these tests are billed in US Dollars and can be expensive in India. Additionally, the scores need to be sent from ETS (the organising institute) to the relevant university, so they charge for it as well. ETS frequently releases discount vouchers for exam registration and score linking, so watch out for them like a hawk. It will save you a LOT of money! However, for MS applications, unfortunately, GRE scores are still a must and heavily weighted. A professor from Georgia Tech I spoke to told me that the first two factors in an MS admission are the GRE score and GPA. GRE scores are relevant for 3 years.

TOEFL is the most universally accepted English proficiency exam for PhD applications and is almost mandatory by all universities, unless waived off. TOEFL has 4 sections (reading, writing, listening, and speaking), all graded out of 30. Each university has a per-section cutoff and an overall cutoff, with most expecting at least 26-28 in speaking for TA enrollment. Otherwise, you may have to take another English course upon admission. TOEFL scores are valid for 2 years.

IELTS is also an acceptable test; however, I have not seen many students take it (except that MIT considers it the primary metric), so I don't have much to say about it.

The most important advice I have here is to start early, and I cannot emphasize this enough. If you are applying for MS/PhD, please complete your exams by June-July to be absolutely safe, with August-September also being an acceptable period. October-November is the most crowded, and you may not find slots to take a test. Plus, you don't want to be distracted during the crucial application phase to study for these tests. TOEFL scores take about a week to be fully prepared, so have a buffer for it. Additionally, linking scores to the university can also take 3-5 business days, so account for it as well, and any hiccups will cost you more time. I highly recommend going to a center to take the Internet-based test, as home tests are heavily proctored and prone to failure due to power and network disruptions, and any issue can cause your score to be withheld.

Mailing Professors Building connections

One of the most crucial aspects of your application process is your connection to the professors you are applying to. It is highly encouraged to email that person and let them know that you are applying and inquire about his/her recruitment. In many cases, professors might be on leave, sabbatical, hiring freeze, or about to move, so they might not wish to take up any new students. Since the professors will be absolutely swamped with such emails during this period, it could be that your email might be missed or ignored, so don't be discouraged. You could potentially drop a follow-up if needed, and if you think you align well with them and their lab, but don't overdo it. Keep an eye out for their personal websites, where they usually mention their hiring status and any follow-up they expect from you. If a certain professor is not recruiting, don't waste your effort on it, and look for others. At the end of the day, you want your application to be recognized by someone who is hiring and can offer you admission.

I have also seen cases where mentors have mailed the professors on behalf of their students, (or introducing them) which has a much higher impact, as it's a direct endorsement. However, this is extremely risky for the mentor as it puts their reputation and recommendation value at stake. Hence, many researchers openly refuse to do it. Only in extremely rare cases, when the mentor has a direct or personal connection with the professor, and strongly believes that the student is a perfect fit, they might consider mailing on their behalf.

If you get a chance to visit a conference around the application period, go for it! Try to meet professors in person and let them know your intentions. Nothing beats a face-to-face and humane conversation to date, and you are more likely to get a response and some additional information. Talk to their students to understand the lab culture and mentors' expectations. I am aware of students who have consistently coordinated with professors before, during, and after the application process, and they did get good offers from them, or were redirected to others who were hiring (which is also quite beneficial).

Letter of Recommendation (LoR) Endorsements from mentors who know your strengths.

This document is the answer to, “How will the professor or Admissions committee know that my work is genuine and I did not fake my SoP and skills?” A Letter of Recommendation from your current or previous mentors/advisors provides the grounding for your SoP and formally introduces you to the reader. I have been told by many that this letter is the most quintessential piece of your package and carries the maximum weightage.

Every PhD application asks for at least 3 LoRs (at most 5), and a general rule of thumb is to have one LoR from your college professor (Master's or Bachelor's), who can also vouch for your academic work. If you think that LoR will not be very strong for some reason, you can skip it. Most LoRs for an application are chosen in a way that the reader knows the writer. This has the highest impact as the professor would be more comfortable in believing a letter from a notable researcher, acquaintance, or close collaborator than otherwise. Most LoRs are hidden from the student and privately written and submitted by the advisors, but in some cases, the advisor might ask you to write your own draft, and they would make changes or submit it as it is. Do talk with your advisor about what they expect from you, how many LoRs they are willing to submit for you, and plan accordingly. Share your SoP with your mentors for them to ground their LoR, as they need to go in tandem. Your advisor might also ask you to share what you expect them to mention in your LoR, so try to keep the information in all LoRs as distinct as possible, i.e., each letter tells the unique experiences of that writer with you and how you worked with them, which is also the reason for multiple LoRs, the multi-faceted approach.

An LoR can be strong or weak depending on how much the writer can vouch for you and has to say about you. The strength of a LoR is decided by its personal touch and how deeply the writer was connected to you. Say, you did a course with a famous professor, and just took their LoR for his/her name based on the course. The professor might write some good/generic things, but it will still be considered weak. Usually, no writer writes negative things about their students in the letters, but a very generic, lukewarm, or unenthusiastic LoR is itself considered bad or weak.

In an unfortunate case where you have to write your own LoR or a draft, here are some tips. Remember, the LoR should be your experiences, but from the writer's perspective. You can spice it up slightly, but don't overdo it and portray yourself as a hero student, as it's easily identifiable and will be given lower value.

  • Your LoR should concur with your SoP in terms of projects, learnings, and findings.
  • Discuss your interactions, i.e., how inquisitive you were, and how driven you were to look up literature, relevant problems, and own the project as much as possible.
  • From that writer's perspective, share your coding and implementation skills, how you overcame roadblocks, and stood by your project even after completion, i.e., drove open-source efforts, tended to the open issues on the repo/model, etc.
  • The letter should introduce you from the writer's perspective in the first paragraph, i.e., who you are in the lab, how long you worked together, on what topics, and what the overall aim of all the projects was.
  • The letter should make the reader believe that you are capable of independent research during a PhD. My Faculty Advisor from IITM once told me that a LoR should sound enthusiastic, i.e., your writer believes in your PhD ambition and is very happy to "strongly recommend" you to that university.
  • Discuss the projects in moderate detail, what your contributions were, where they were finally published, and any awards and notable achievements (quickly garnered citations or downloads). Highlight any additional duties you did during your time with the writer, i.e., mentoring students, TA duties, taking any classes, etc. These should be at the end as additional information and will highlight your overall skills. You can also end with what you wish to do in your PhD, again from the perspective of the writer with whom you might have discussed this.
  • Very important: Make sure the LoR is dated with a date not more than 1 month ago from the submission, and is preferably on an official letterhead; some universities are specific about it. Send all the LoR requests to the writer's official email ID. Some universities don't allow personal IDs or ask for further verification for them. Try to keep the LoR 2-3 pages. It should be detailed enough to make the reader understand and believe what the mentor saw in you to recommend you. Don't use AI to write it, and keep the letter as humane as possible. You can look up sample LoRs and their formats on the web, and some Overleaf templates might also exist. But it would be better to follow the mentor's institutional letterhead.
Application Fees & Expenses Financial considerations and support options.

The application process can be quite expensive, with each university charging 50-120 USD per application. Coupled with that is the cost of linking scores to the universities from ETS. However, universities also provide subsidies and fee-waivers to students. Here, you may have to explain your financial situation and also provide necessary documents to corroborate your case. But these fee-waivers are usually first-come-first-serve and limited in number. Your case and document-verification from the university might take a week (or even longer closer to the deadline), so complete them early to avoid last minute rush. Note, you don't need to submit the application for the waiver. It can be done independently, and you will be notified if you are granted one along with the subsequent steps. Generally, you are not expected to submit the application until the fee waiver is decided.

Additionally, ETS allows you to link your TOEFL/GRE scores to 4 universities right after the exam for free. So, memorize some university and department codes to pre-link them and save money.

Curriculum Vitae (CV) Academic and professional accomplishments.

A CV serves as a quick introduction of you, your history, and your achievements. There are several CV templates, and you can follow one of them. Do list your publications in a specific section. All the achievements and accolades, and community service (not social work, but some service to the research community, such as co-organizing a conference/workshop/tutorial, reviewing for conferences, etc) go in another. Your CV should also provide a quick history of your education, along with your GPA (any university rank you achieved), and your work experiences. You may choose to provide 1-3 lines about some notable/recent/relevant projects with their major findings and notable achievements, but don't overdo it and write paragraphs. This is just to provide the reader with a quick glimpse of what you did.

Safe Schools A game of strategy and safety.

A big mistake I made during my application cycle was to not apply to safe schools. Safe schools are those universities where are almost sure of getting an admit, either due to your relatively stronger profile or a direct understanding with a professor. Given the competition in AI and NLP, I strongly suggest to maintain a gradient in your applications, i.e. 20% top schools, 40%-50% mid range and 30-40% safe schools. You can vary this ratio based your discussion with your mentors or seniors who can assess your profile. Only applying to top schools can be super risky due to their high profile requirement and competition, and your chances of getting an admit there are generally lower. If you are absolutely sure you will rejected at University XYZ, don't apply to it and waste your time, effort, and money, but instead choose a mid-range or safe school. After all, your main aim should be to get a shortlist/interview call.

Formally, any sort of preference or bias you add to your application process will narrow your sample space and in turn, your successful events. MS/PhD applications are, at some level, a game of probability. Assume you have a success probability of p. So, if you apply to N schools, you can expect to receive offers from Np universities. Now, if you reduce N, your expected return will also be lower.

Take a break! Rest and recharge during the application process.

The entire application cycle can be mentally taxing, and you will see everyone around you who applied equally worried and anxious about the interview calls and results. You will find people discussing who got interviews from a university and from the professor you applied to, and it builds a ton of uncertainty. So, if you get a chance, take a break during this period. Go home and relax. Do not engage in those anxious conversations if they're not relevant.
